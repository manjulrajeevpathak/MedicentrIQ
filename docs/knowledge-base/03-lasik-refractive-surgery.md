# LASIK & Refractive Surgery (Freedom from Glasses)

## Summary for prompt

LASIK and related procedures (PRK, SMILE, ICL) surgically correct glasses
power in adults. Candidates are generally 18+ with a stable power for about
a year, healthy corneas, and no active eye disease; a detailed pre-LASIK
work-up (corneal scans) decides eligibility and which procedure fits.
LASIK/SMILE reshape the cornea with a laser in minutes with rapid recovery;
PRK is a surface laser suited to thinner corneas (slower recovery); ICL is
a lens implanted inside the eye for very high powers or unsuitable corneas.
All are daycare procedures. After 40, reading glasses will still be needed
eventually (presbyopia). If a patient asks "can my glasses go?", invite
them for a refractive work-up.

## Who is a candidate for LASIK?

Typical requirements: age 18 or older; glasses power stable (no significant
change for ~12 months); power within treatable range (commonly up to about
−8 to −10 D myopia, +4 to +5 D hyperopia, and up to ~5 D cylinder,
depending on corneal thickness); healthy cornea with adequate thickness and
normal shape on scanning (no keratoconus); no active eye infection, severe
dry eye, uncontrolled diabetes or certain autoimmune conditions; and not
currently pregnant or nursing (power fluctuates). The pre-LASIK work-up —
corneal topography/tomography, thickness mapping, dry-eye and retina checks
— is what actually decides candidacy and the safest procedure. Some
patients are better served by PRK or ICL instead; a good work-up sometimes
says "no surgery", which protects the eye.

## The procedures compared

- **LASIK** — a thin corneal flap is created (with a femtosecond laser in
  "bladeless" LASIK), the excimer laser reshapes the cornea underneath, and
  the flap is repositioned. Takes ~10–15 minutes for both eyes; vision is
  quite good by the next day; mild dryness for some weeks is common.
- **PRK / surface ablation** — no flap; the surface layer is removed and
  the same laser reshapes the cornea. Preferred for thinner corneas or
  certain professions/sports. Vision recovers over days-to-weeks with more
  initial discomfort, but long-term results match LASIK, with no flap.
- **SMILE** — a femtosecond laser cuts a thin lens-shaped piece (lenticule)
  inside the cornea, removed through a small keyhole incision. Flapless,
  small incision, possibly less dry eye; treats myopia and myopic
  astigmatism.
- **ICL (Implantable Collamer Lens)** — a soft lens is placed INSIDE the
  eye, in front of the natural lens, without removing any tissue. Suited to
  very high powers (e.g., beyond LASIK range) and thin/abnormal corneas.
  Reversible in principle (the lens can be removed); it is an intraocular
  surgery, so the safety profile is that of a lens implant rather than a
  laser reshaping.

| Procedure | Best for | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| LASIK | Normal corneas, moderate powers | Fast, nearly painless recovery | Flap-related precautions; dryness |
| PRK | Thinner corneas, contact-sport users | No flap, same long-term result | Slower, sorer recovery |
| SMILE | Myopia ± cylinder | Flapless keyhole, less dryness | Myopia only; surgeon-dependent |
| ICL | Very high power, thin cornea | Treats extreme powers; removable | Inside-the-eye surgery; costlier |

## What surgery day and recovery look like

All options are daycare procedures with numbing drops — no injections, no
hospital stay. The laser itself works for seconds per eye. Expect watering,
light-sensitivity and a gritty feeling for a day (longer with PRK). Most
LASIK/SMILE patients see well enough to work on screens within 1–2 days.
Precautions for the early weeks: don't rub the eyes, avoid dusty/smoky
places, no swimming for ~2–4 weeks, no eye makeup for ~1–2 weeks, and use
the prescribed drop schedule (antibiotic + lubricants). Follow-ups are
typically day 1, week 1, and month 1.

### Q: Is LASIK safe? Can I go blind?
A: LASIK is one of the most performed and studied elective surgeries, with
a very high satisfaction rate. Serious sight-threatening complications are
rare, especially with a proper pre-operative work-up (which screens out
unsuitable corneas). Known issues include temporary dryness, glare/halos at
night (usually settling over months), and under/over-correction that may
need an enhancement. The work-up conversation with the surgeon covers
personal risk honestly.

### Q: Will my power come back after LASIK?
A: The treated power is permanently removed from the cornea. A small number
of patients have mild regression over years, and everyone still develops
normal age-related reading power (presbyopia) after 40, plus cataract in
later life as usual. LASIK does not prevent or cause these; it also doesn't
block future cataract surgery (mention past LASIK during any later eye
surgery work-up).

### Q: LASIK vs ICL — how do I choose?
A: If the cornea is healthy and thick enough and the power is in range,
laser options (LASIK/SMILE/PRK) are simplest. If the power is very high or
the cornea is thin or irregular, ICL is often the better and safer route.
The scans decide — that's why the work-up comes first.
