# Red Eye, Conjunctivitis, Allergy, Dry Eye & Uveitis

## Summary for prompt

Most red eyes are conjunctivitis ("eye flu" — sticky, watery, gritty, both
eyes, vision normal) or allergy (itching dominant, seasonal, both eyes) —
uncomfortable but rarely dangerous, managed with hygiene, cold compresses
and prescribed drops. DANGER signs that make a red eye urgent: eye PAIN,
light-sensitivity, reduced vision, coloured halos, a white spot on the
cornea, contact-lens use, injury or chemical exposure, or a red eye in a
newborn. Uveitis is inflammation INSIDE the eye (painful, light-sensitive
red eye with blur; often recurrent, sometimes linked to body-wide disease)
— it needs specialist treatment, not over-the-counter drops. Never
self-use steroid drops. Dry eye causes burning, grittiness and tired
screen-time eyes; it is managed, not cured, with lubricants and habits.

## Conjunctivitis ("eye flu" / aankh aana)

Infective conjunctivitis — viral (most common, often with a cold, spreads
easily) or bacterial (stickier, yellow discharge) — causes red, watery,
gritty eyes with crusting on waking, usually starting in one eye and
spreading to the other. Vision stays essentially normal. It typically runs
its course in 1–2 weeks; care is symptomatic: cold compresses, frequent
hand-washing, own towel/pillow, no eye-rubbing, discard eye makeup, avoid
swimming, and prescribed drops when needed. It spreads by touch (hands,
towels) — NOT by simply looking at someone. School/work: stay home while
the eye is actively watering/discharging. See a doctor promptly if there is
pain, light-sensitivity, blurred vision, severe swelling, or no improvement
within a few days — and always for babies under one month (newborn
conjunctivitis is treated urgently).

## Allergic eye disease (itching-dominant)

Allergy makes eyes ITCH intensely, water, and look pink and swollen, often
seasonally or with dust/pollen exposure, usually BOTH eyes, and frequently
with sneezing. In children a chronic form (vernal keratoconjunctivitis,
"spring catarrh") causes intense itching, thick mucus and light-
sensitivity, and needs specialist care — badly rubbed allergic eyes also
raise the risk of keratoconus (see cornea document). Management: avoid
triggers, COLD compresses, preservative-free lubricants, prescribed
anti-allergy drops; steroid drops help severe flares but only ever under
doctor supervision, because unsupervised steroids cause cataract and
glaucoma. The single best habit: STOP eye rubbing.

## Dry eye disease

Dry eye is a poor or unstable tear film: burning, grittiness, sandy
feeling, red rims, blurred vision that clears on blinking, and — counter-
intuitively — episodes of watering. Big drivers: screen time (blink rate
halves at screens), AC/fan exposure, contact lenses, ageing/menopause,
certain medicines, and lid-margin oil-gland disease (blepharitis).
Management is ongoing, not one-shot: preservative-free lubricating drops
used regularly (not just when it hurts), the 20-20-20 screen rule (every
20 minutes look 20 feet away for 20 seconds), warm compresses + lid
hygiene for blepharitis, wrap-around glasses outdoors, and prescription
anti-inflammatory drops or procedures for stubborn disease. Severe pain or
vision drop is NOT typical dry eye — get examined.

## Uveitis — inflammation inside the eye

Uveitis is inflammation of the eye's inner vascular layer: a deeply aching,
red, light-sensitive eye with tearing and blurred vision, usually WITHOUT
discharge — quite different from conjunctivitis. It can be one-off,
recurrent or chronic; some cases associate with joint/back inflammation,
certain infections (including TB in India), or autoimmune disease, so
work-up may include blood tests and physician review. Treatment is
prescription anti-inflammatory therapy (steroid drops with dilating drops,
and stepped-up medicines for severe/recurrent disease) with close
monitoring — untreated or self-treated uveitis causes glaucoma, cataract
and permanent damage. A patient with a KNOWN uveitis history whose typical
pain/redness returns should be seen within a day — early treatment of a
flare protects vision.

### Q: Which drops should I put for a red eye?
A: The bot should not name medicines. Safe general steps: stop contact-lens
use, don't rub, use cold compresses, and wash hands often. Because a few
red-eye causes are serious — and steroid drops without examination can be
harmful — a red eye with pain, light-sensitivity, blur, injury, chemical
splash or lens use needs prompt examination; a simple sticky red eye that
isn't settling in 2–3 days should also be seen.
