# Diabetic Retinopathy (Diabetes and the Eye)

## Summary for prompt

Diabetes damages the tiny blood vessels of the retina (the light-sensitive
"screen" at the back of the eye). This is diabetic retinopathy — the
leading cause of preventable blindness in working-age adults. Early stages
have NO symptoms, so every diabetic needs a dilated retina check at least
once a year even with perfect vision. Treatments (injections, laser,
surgery) work best BEFORE vision drops. Good control of blood sugar, blood
pressure and cholesterol slows the disease. If a diabetic patient reports
blurring, floaters, or any sudden vision change — book a retina evaluation
promptly; if vision dropped suddenly, treat it as urgent.

## How diabetes affects the eye

Years of high blood sugar weaken the walls of the retina's fine blood
vessels. They leak fluid and fats (causing swelling), bleed, or close off —
and the oxygen-starved retina then grows fragile new vessels that bleed
easily and scar. The two sight-threatening patterns are **diabetic macular
oedema** (swelling at the central reading point of the retina — blurs
central vision) and **proliferative diabetic retinopathy** (new abnormal
vessels — can cause sudden bleeding inside the eye or retinal detachment).
Diabetes also brings earlier cataract and a higher glaucoma risk, so the
annual check covers the whole eye.

## Stages and symptoms

Early (non-proliferative) retinopathy usually causes NO symptoms — the
patient sees perfectly while damage accumulates. As it advances: gradual
blurring, patchy or fluctuating vision (also varies with sugar levels),
faded colours, floaters (spots/cobwebs), difficulty reading. A sudden
shower of floaters or sudden dark/blocked vision suggests bleeding inside
the eye (vitreous haemorrhage) or retinal detachment — urgent.

**Key message the bot should repeat:** good vision today does NOT mean the
retina is fine. Only a dilated examination or retina imaging can tell.

## Screening: who and how often

- Type 2 diabetes: retina check at diagnosis, then at least yearly.
- Type 1 diabetes: within 5 years of diagnosis, then yearly.
- Pregnancy with diabetes: check in early pregnancy and as advised —
  pregnancy can speed retinopathy up.
- More frequent (3–6 monthly) once any retinopathy is present, as advised.

The check involves dilating drops (vision blurs for a few hours — don't
drive back yourself) and examination/photography of the retina; OCT scans
measure macular swelling. Painless throughout.

## Treatment options

- **Control of sugar, BP and cholesterol** — the foundation. HbA1c near the
  personal target, blood pressure controlled, no smoking. This slows every
  stage.
- **Intravitreal injections (anti-VEGF)** — tiny painless injections into
  the eye that dry up macular swelling and shrink new vessels; usually a
  series of injections over months, with monitoring. The mainstay for
  macular oedema.
- **Laser (PRP / focal)** — seals leaks and treats oxygen-starved retina to
  prevent bleeding complications in proliferative disease; preserves rather
  than restores vision.
- **Vitrectomy surgery** — for non-clearing bleeding or retinal detachment;
  removes blood and scar tissue from inside the eye.

### Q: Are eye injections painful or dangerous?
A: The eye is numbed with drops; most patients feel pressure, not pain, and
the injection itself takes seconds. It is a routine, widely used treatment.
Minor redness or a floater for a day is common; severe pain or dropping
vision after an injection is NOT normal — return immediately.

### Q: My sugar is controlled now — can retinopathy still worsen?
A: Established retinopathy can progress even after control improves, which
is why scheduled retina follow-ups continue. But good control markedly
slows damage and improves every treatment's results — it is never wasted.

### Q: Can diabetic retinopathy be cured?
A: Damaged retina cannot be made new, but timely treatment prevents most
blindness from diabetes. The realistic goals are: protect what you have,
recover what swelling allows, and never miss the window where treatment
works. Screening is how the window is caught.

## Red flags for diabetic patients — come urgently

Sudden shower of floaters, sudden dark patch or curtain in the vision,
sudden drop in vision in one eye, or eye pain with redness. These may mean
bleeding, detachment or high eye pressure — same-day or next-morning
evaluation, not "wait and watch".
