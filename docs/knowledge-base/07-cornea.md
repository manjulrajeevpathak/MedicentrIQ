# Cornea — Keratoconus, Infections, Transplant

## Summary for prompt

The cornea is the clear front window of the eye. Key corneal conditions:
keratoconus (progressive thinning/bulging in young people — frequent
power changes, especially cylinder; stabilised early with C3R
cross-linking), corneal ulcers/infections (painful red eye, often
contact-lens or injury related — an emergency), and corneal scarring or
failure treated by corneal transplant (full or layered, using donated eye
tissue — which is why eye donation matters). A young patient whose cylinder
power keeps rising needs a corneal scan; a painful red eye in a
contact-lens user must be seen the same day.

## Keratoconus — the weak, bulging cornea

Keratoconus usually starts in the teens/20s: the cornea progressively
thins and bulges into a cone shape, causing rising astigmatism (cylinder),
frequent prescription changes, ghosting/smearing of images, and glare.
Vigorous eye rubbing and allergic eye disease are linked to progression —
stop rubbing is genuine medical advice here. Diagnosis is by corneal
topography/tomography scans (which also screen every LASIK candidate).

Management by stage: glasses → specialty contact lenses (rigid/scleral)
for clear vision; **C3R / corneal collagen cross-linking** to STOP
progression (a ~30-minute procedure using riboflavin drops and UV light —
it stiffens the cornea; it stabilises rather than improves vision);
rings/segments in selected cases; and corneal transplant for advanced
scarring. The message for young patients: detected early and cross-linked
in time, most keratoconus patients never need a transplant.

### Q: My cylinder number keeps increasing every few months — is that normal?
A: Rapidly rising cylinder power in a young person is a red flag for
keratoconus and deserves a corneal scan (topography). It's a quick,
painless test — and catching keratoconus early means it can be stabilised
before vision quality is permanently affected.

## Corneal infections and ulcers — an emergency

A corneal ulcer is an infection of the front window of the eye: a painful
red eye, watering, light-sensitivity, a white spot on the black of the eye,
and dropping vision. Common triggers: contact-lens misuse (sleeping/
showering/swimming in lenses, tap water rinsing), injury with vegetable
matter (a real risk during harvest season — fungal ulcers), or a scratched
eye that got worse. **This is a same-day emergency**: corneal infections
can scar or perforate within days. Treatment is intensive antimicrobial
drops after the doctor scrapes a tiny sample; steroid drops must NEVER be
self-started on a red eye, as they can make an infection catastrophically
worse. Healed ulcers can leave scars; dense central scars may later need
transplant.

## Corneal transplant (keratoplasty) and eye donation

When the cornea is permanently scarred, swollen or failed, sight can be
restored by replacing it with clear donated corneal tissue. Options:
**full-thickness transplant (PK)** and modern **layered (lamellar)
transplants** — DALK (replaces front layers, e.g., for keratoconus scars)
and DSEK/DMEK (replaces only the inner pump layer, e.g., for corneal
swelling after surgery) — layered surgery keeps more of the patient's own
cornea, with faster recovery and lower rejection risk. After transplant:
long-term steroid drops as prescribed, gradual vision recovery over months,
and lifelong awareness of REJECTION warning signs — Redness, Sensitivity to
light, Vision drop, Pain (RSVP): any of these means come immediately, as
early rejection is usually reversible with treatment.

**Eye donation:** corneas can be donated after death (within hours),
regardless of age or spectacle power in most cases, and one donor gives
sight to two people. Cataract, diabetes and most illnesses do NOT prevent
donation. Families can pledge and, at the time of loss, call the nearest
eye bank — the bot can share the hospital's eye-bank contact if configured.

## Dry, exposed and injured corneas

Severe dry eye, incomplete lid closure, chemical burns and injuries all
threaten the cornea. Chemical splash (acid/alkali, cleaning agents, lime/
chuna) is the one situation where treatment starts BEFORE coming: wash the
open eye immediately with large amounts of clean water for 15–30 minutes,
then come urgently. Metallic foreign bodies, welding flashes and
fingernail scratches also need prompt care — most heal fully when treated
early.
