# Cataract (Motiyabind)

## Summary for prompt

A cataract is clouding of the eye's natural lens, most often due to ageing.
It causes gradually blurring or hazy vision, glare at night, and frequent
changes in glasses power. Cataract cannot be cured by drops, glasses, diet
or exercises — surgery is the only treatment, and it is one of the safest
and most successful surgeries in all of medicine. Surgery replaces the
cloudy lens with a clear artificial lens (IOL). Modern phacoemulsification
("Phaco") surgery needs no injection, no stitches and no bandage, takes
about 10–20 minutes, and most patients see clearly within a few days. A
cataract does NOT need to "ripen" — waiting too long actually makes surgery
harder. If a patient describes painless, gradually worsening blur, glare
while driving at night, or colours looking faded, suggest a cataract
check-up.

## What is a cataract?

A cataract is the clouding of the natural lens inside the eye. The lens
sits behind the coloured part of the eye (iris) and focuses light onto the
retina. With age — and sometimes due to diabetes, injury, steroid use,
smoking or heredity — the lens proteins clump together and turn the lens
cloudy, like a frosted window. Light can no longer pass through clearly, so
vision becomes blurred, dim or hazy. A cataract is not a growth, film or
skin over the eye, and it does not spread from one eye to the other,
although both eyes often develop cataracts around the same time.

## Symptoms of cataract

Common symptoms of cataract include:
- Gradual, painless blurring or dimming of vision
- Glare and halos around lights, especially while driving at night
- Colours appearing faded or yellowish
- Frequent changes in spectacle power; new glasses stop helping
- Needing brighter light to read
- Double vision or ghost images in one eye

Cataract is painless. Eye pain with redness is NOT typical of cataract and
needs an urgent eye examination for other causes.

## Who gets cataract, and can it be prevented?

Age is the biggest cause — most people above 60 have some degree of
cataract, and many develop it in their 50s. Cataracts appear earlier in
people with diabetes, high myopia, long-term steroid use (tablets, drops or
inhalers), previous eye injury or surgery, heavy smoking, and significant
sunlight (UV) exposure. Some babies are born with cataract (congenital
cataract), which needs urgent paediatric eye care. No medicine, eye drop,
food or exercise has been proven to prevent or reverse cataract. Sunglasses,
good diabetes control and not smoking may slow it down. The only treatment
is surgery, done when the cataract starts interfering with daily life.

## When should cataract be operated?

There is no need to wait for the cataract to "ripen" or "mature" — that is
an old belief. Surgery is advised when blurred vision starts affecting the
patient's daily activities: reading, driving, recognising faces, working,
watching TV, or moving about safely. Operating at the right time is easier
and safer; a very advanced ("hypermature") cataract becomes hard, needs
more ultrasound energy, has a higher complication risk, and can even cause
pain or glaucoma if neglected. If a doctor has advised surgery, delaying by
months usually only makes vision worse in the meantime.

## Types of cataract surgery

**Phacoemulsification (Phaco)** — the modern standard. Through a tiny 2–3 mm
opening, an ultrasound probe breaks the cloudy lens into small pieces and
removes them, and a foldable artificial lens (IOL) is placed. No injection
(numbing is by eye drops), no stitches, no bandage in most cases. Fast
recovery, usually within days.

**MSICS (Manual Small-Incision Cataract Surgery)** — a slightly larger
(~6 mm) self-sealing incision through which the lens is removed in one
piece, usually with a non-foldable lens. Very safe and effective, often used
for very hard/advanced cataracts or in high-volume charitable settings.
Recovery is slightly slower than Phaco and astigmatism can be a little
higher.

**FLACS (Femtosecond Laser-Assisted Cataract Surgery)** — a laser performs
some steps (incisions, opening the lens capsule, softening the lens) before
Phaco completes the removal. Adds precision for selected cases and premium
lens implants, at a higher cost. Final visual results for routine cataracts
are broadly similar to standard Phaco.

**ECCE (conventional large-incision surgery)** — an older technique with a
large stitched incision and long recovery. Rarely used today except in
special situations.

### Q: Phaco vs older surgery — why is Phaco better?
A: Phaco uses a much smaller cut, numbing drops instead of an injection
around the eye, no stitches and usually no bandage. Patients walk in and
walk out the same day and most resume normal activities within a few days.
Older large-incision techniques need an injection, stitches, a bandage and
weeks of recovery, and cause more astigmatism (cylinder power) afterwards.

## Intraocular lenses (IOLs) — the implant choices

During cataract surgery the cloudy natural lens is replaced with a clear
artificial lens called an intraocular lens (IOL). It stays in the eye for
life and needs no maintenance. Main types:

- **Monofocal IOL** — clear focus at ONE distance, usually far. Reading
  glasses are needed for near work. The most proven, most affordable
  option; excellent night-vision quality.
- **Monofocal Toric IOL** — a monofocal that also corrects astigmatism
  (cylinder power). For patients with significant corneal astigmatism, a
  toric lens gives much sharper distance vision without glasses.
- **Multifocal / Trifocal IOL** — divides light to give far, intermediate
  and near focus, greatly reducing dependence on glasses. Trade-offs: some
  patients notice halos/glare around lights at night and slightly reduced
  contrast; not ideal for people with retinal disease, advanced glaucoma or
  very high visual demands at night (e.g., night drivers).
- **EDOF (Extended Depth of Focus) IOL** — a newer design giving a
  continuous range from far to intermediate (computer distance) with fewer
  halos than multifocals; thin reading glasses may still be needed for fine
  print.
- **Multifocal/EDOF Toric** — the above designs combined with astigmatism
  correction.

The right lens depends on the eye's health (retina, cornea, astigmatism),
the patient's lifestyle (driving, reading, screen work) and budget. The
surgeon measures the eye (biometry) and recommends options — the bot should
explain categories and let the doctor advise the specific lens.

### Q: Which lens is best?
A: There is no single "best" lens — a monofocal gives the most dependable
quality of vision with reading glasses; trifocal/EDOF lenses reduce
dependence on glasses but can cause night halos in some patients; toric
versions correct cylinder power. The surgeon recommends based on eye
measurements and lifestyle. All modern IOLs last a lifetime.

### Q: Foldable vs non-foldable lens?
A: A foldable lens can be inserted through the tiny Phaco incision (2–3 mm),
so the cut stays small and heals fast without stitches. Non-foldable (rigid)
lenses need a larger incision, as in MSICS. Foldable lenses are standard in
Phaco surgery.

## Pros and cons at a glance

| Choice | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Phaco + foldable IOL | Tiny cut, drops-only anaesthesia, no stitch/bandage, fastest recovery | Very hard cataracts may still need MSICS |
| MSICS | Robust for very advanced cataracts, cost-effective, proven | Larger cut, slower recovery, slightly more astigmatism |
| FLACS | Laser precision for select steps, useful with premium IOLs | Costlier; routine outcomes similar to Phaco |
| Monofocal IOL | Best-proven optics, crisp night vision, affordable | Reading glasses needed |
| Toric IOL | Corrects astigmatism, sharper unaided distance vision | Costlier; needs precise alignment |
| Trifocal IOL | Far + computer + reading mostly glasses-free | Night halos/glare possible; not for every eye |
| EDOF IOL | Smooth far-to-intermediate range, fewer halos | Fine print may still need thin readers |

## Before surgery — what to expect

- A full eye check-up and **biometry** (painless scans to calculate lens
  power), plus checks of the retina and eye pressure.
- Blood pressure and blood sugar should be reasonably controlled; routine
  medicines are usually continued — the team will advise about blood
  thinners.
- Surgery is a daycare procedure: the patient goes home the same day. Come
  with a companion, avoid eye makeup, and follow the fasting/food advice
  given by the team.
- The eye is dilated with drops before surgery; anaesthesia is usually just
  numbing drops.

## During and after surgery — recovery timeline

The surgery itself typically takes 10–20 minutes and is painless; the
patient is awake and sees lights and colours, not the instruments. After
Phaco most patients notice clearer vision within 24–72 hours, with vision
stabilising over 2–4 weeks.

Aftercare basics:
- Use the prescribed eye drops exactly on schedule (usually 3–4 weeks).
- Do not rub or press the eye; wear the protective shield while sleeping
  for the first week.
- Avoid water splashing directly into the eye for about a week; head bath
  as advised by the team.
- Avoid dusty environments, swimming and eye makeup for 2–4 weeks.
- Normal walking, watching TV, phone use and light desk work are fine
  within a day or two; heavy lifting/straining as the doctor advises.
- New glasses (if needed) are usually prescribed after 3–4 weeks, when the
  power stabilises.

### Q: Is cataract surgery painful? Will I be awake?
A: The surgery is essentially painless. Numbing eye drops are used, so
there is no injection in most Phaco cases. The patient is awake, sees some
light and movement, but does not see the surgery itself. Mild irritation,
watering or a foreign-body feeling for a day or two afterwards is normal.

### Q: When can I resume work / driving / prayers / cooking?
A: Most people resume desk work, TV and phone in 1–2 days, and normal
routines in about a week. Driving is usually fine once the doctor confirms
vision at the first follow-up. Kitchen work is fine early — just avoid
smoke, steam and dust directly in the eye for the first week or two.

## Risks and possible complications

Cataract surgery is among the safest surgeries, with a very high success
rate. As with any surgery, rare complications can occur: infection
(endophthalmitis — very rare but serious), swelling of the cornea or
retina, bleeding, retained lens fragments, retinal detachment (higher risk
in very high myopia), or a rise in eye pressure. Most are treatable when
caught early — which is why follow-up visits matter.

A common LATE event (months to years later) is **posterior capsule
opacification (PCO)** — the thin membrane behind the implant turns cloudy
and vision dims again. This is NOT the cataract returning; it is cleared in
minutes with a painless laser (YAG capsulotomy) in the clinic.

### Q: Can a cataract come back after surgery?
A: No. The cloudy lens is removed permanently and the artificial lens does
not develop cataract. If vision becomes hazy months or years later, the
usual cause is PCO — a cloudy membrane behind the lens — fixed with a quick,
painless laser in the OPD.

## Warning signs after surgery — contact the hospital immediately

Call or come to the hospital the same day if, after cataract surgery, there
is: increasing pain not relieved by the prescribed drops, worsening redness,
sudden drop in vision, a shower of new floaters or flashes of light, a
curtain/shadow over the vision, or discharge from the eye. These can signal
infection or retinal problems where fast treatment protects sight.

## Both eyes, diabetes, and other special situations

- **Both eyes:** surgeries are usually done a few days to weeks apart, not
  the same day, so the first eye can be checked before the second.
- **Diabetes:** cataract surgery is safe with reasonably controlled sugar;
  the retina is checked first because diabetic retinopathy may also need
  treatment.
- **Glaucoma patients:** cataract surgery is possible and sometimes even
  helps pressure control; the surgeon plans drops accordingly.
- **After LASIK:** cataract surgery is possible; lens power calculation
  needs the extra corneal data, so mention past LASIK during work-up.
- **Congenital cataract in a child:** needs urgent evaluation — delayed
  treatment can permanently limit visual development.
