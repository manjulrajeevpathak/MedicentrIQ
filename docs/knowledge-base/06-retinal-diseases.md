# Retinal Diseases (Parda) — Detachment, ARMD, Vein Occlusion, Macular Hole

## Summary for prompt

The retina ("parda") is the light-sensing film at the back of the eye.
Serious retinal conditions include retinal detachment (an emergency —
flashes, floaters, then a curtain over vision), age-related macular
degeneration/ARMD (central vision distortion or blur after 50; the "wet"
form needs urgent injections), retinal vein occlusion (sudden painless
blurring, linked to BP/diabetes), and macular hole (central distortion,
treated by vitrectomy surgery). Common thread: SUDDEN new floaters,
flashes, a curtain/shadow, or new distortion of straight lines all deserve
urgent retina evaluation — early treatment protects sight. High myopes and
diabetics need scheduled retina checks even without symptoms.

## Floaters and flashes — when normal, when urgent

Occasional small floaters (dots/cobwebs drifting in vision) are common,
especially with age or myopia, as the eye's jelly (vitreous) liquefies.
Usually harmless. **Urgent evaluation (within 24 hours) is needed when:**
a sudden SHOWER of new floaters appears; flashes of light (like lightning
streaks) keep occurring; a dark curtain/shadow starts covering part of the
vision; or vision drops. These can mean a retinal tear — which is sealed
with a simple laser in minutes IF caught before it becomes a detachment.

## Retinal detachment (parda hatna)

Retinal detachment means the retina has peeled off the wall of the eye,
like wallpaper coming off — the detached part stops seeing, and untreated
detachment leads to blindness in that eye. Warning sequence: flashes and a
shower of floaters (tear stage), then a spreading curtain/shadow, then
central vision loss. Risk is higher with high myopia (minus power), after
eye injury, after previous detachment in either eye, and with certain
retinal thinning (lattice degeneration). **Treatment is surgical** —
vitrectomy and/or scleral buckle, with gas or silicone-oil support inside
the eye. With gas, the patient must maintain a specific head posture for
days and MUST NOT fly or travel to high altitude until the gas absorbs.
Visual result depends heavily on whether the central retina (macula) was
still attached at surgery — which is why hours matter at the curtain stage.

### Q: Can a detached retina be fixed with laser or drops?
A: A retinal TEAR (early stage) can be sealed with laser in the OPD. Once
the retina has DETACHED, only surgery can reattach it — no drops, medicines
or laser alone will work. That's why acting at the flashes/floaters stage
is so valuable.

## Age-related macular degeneration (ARMD/AMD)

ARMD affects the macula — the central point of the retina used for reading
and recognising faces — usually after age 50. **Dry ARMD** (the common
form) progresses slowly: gradual central blur, needing brighter light;
managed with monitoring, specific antioxidant supplements when advised,
and lifestyle measures (no smoking, healthy diet with green leafy
vegetables and fish). **Wet ARMD** is when fragile new vessels leak under
the macula: straight lines look bent/wavy, a central smudge or grey patch
appears, and vision can fall within weeks — treated with a course of
anti-VEGF injections, best started early. Side (peripheral) vision is
preserved in ARMD — patients do not go completely blind, but central tasks
suffer. Patients can self-monitor at home with an **Amsler grid** (checking
each eye separately for wavy or missing lines) and must report new
distortion immediately.

### Q: One eye has ARMD — what about the other?
A: The second eye carries a real risk over the following years, so
scheduled checks plus weekly Amsler-grid self-testing of the good eye are
important. New distortion in either eye = prompt visit.

## Retinal vein occlusion (RVO — "eye stroke of the vein")

A blocked retinal vein causes sudden, painless blurring or a patch of lost
vision in one eye, often discovered on waking. It is strongly associated
with high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, and glaucoma. Vision
drops mainly from macular swelling, treated with anti-VEGF injections
(often a series), sometimes laser; severe cases are watched for
pressure-raising new vessels. Equally important: a physician check-up of
BP, sugar, lipids and heart risk — the eye event is a warning about the
body's circulation.

## Macular hole and epiretinal membrane

A **macular hole** is a small gap that forms at the central retina, usually
age-related: straight lines bend, a small central blank spot appears, and
reading blurs — treated with vitrectomy surgery and a gas bubble
(with some face-down posturing), with good closure rates when operated
early. An **epiretinal membrane** ("macular pucker") is a fine scar tissue
film that wrinkles the macula, causing distortion and blur; surgery peels
the membrane when vision is affected enough to matter to the patient.

## Retina surgery (vitrectomy) — what patients should know

Vitrectomy removes the eye's jelly through microscopic ports to repair
detachment, remove blood, peel membranes or close macular holes. It is
usually done under local anaesthesia as a daycare/overnight procedure. If a
gas bubble is placed: vision stays poor while the bubble absorbs
(days–weeks), a specific posture may be needed, and flying/mountain travel
is forbidden until cleared. Cataract commonly develops or advances after
vitrectomy in older patients and is dealt with later. Recovery of vision
depends on the underlying disease — the surgeon sets expectations
case-by-case.
