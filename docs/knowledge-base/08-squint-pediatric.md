# Squint (Bhengapan), Lazy Eye & Children's Eye Care

## Summary for prompt

A squint (strabismus) is misalignment of the eyes — one eye turns in, out,
up or down. In children it is never "just cosmetic" and never something to
outgrow: an ignored squint can cause permanent lazy eye (amblyopia),
because the child's brain switches off the deviating eye. Treatment works
best in early childhood — glasses, patching therapy, and/or muscle surgery.
Any parent reporting a turning eye, head tilt, closing one eye in
sunlight, or a white reflection in a child's pupil in photos should be
booked promptly; a NEW squint or double vision in an ADULT needs urgent
neurological-cause evaluation. Children can be examined at ANY age —
including infants.

## What is squint and why it matters in children

Squint means the two eyes do not point at the same object together. In a
child, the developing brain deals with the double image by suppressing the
turning eye — and an eye the brain keeps ignoring never learns to see
(amblyopia/lazy eye). Vision development is mostly complete by age 8–10,
so squint and lazy eye are time-critical: treated early, most children gain
straight eyes AND good vision in both; treated late, the alignment can
still be fixed but the weak eye's vision may remain permanently poor.
Squints can be constant or intermittent, and some are caused purely by
uncorrected glasses power (accommodative squint) — those may straighten
with glasses alone.

## Lazy eye (amblyopia) and patching

Amblyopia is reduced vision in a structurally normal eye because the brain
did not learn to use it — caused by squint, a large difference in power
between the two eyes, or anything blocking vision in infancy (droopy lid,
congenital cataract). Treatment: correct the cause (glasses first), then
force the brain to use the weak eye — **patching** the good eye for
prescribed hours daily (or blurring drops as an alternative). Patching
succeeds when done consistently and early; parents should expect weeks-to-
months of therapy with progress checks. Older children can still improve,
but response falls with age — hence the urgency.

### Q: My child's eye turns only sometimes / only in photos — should I wait?
A: No. Even an intermittent squint deserves an examination — and a photo
where one pupil glows WHITE (instead of the usual red) is a separate,
serious red flag (it can indicate congenital cataract or, rarely,
retinoblastoma — an eye cancer of childhood) requiring an urgent visit.
Bring such photos along.

### Q: Will my child outgrow the squint?
A: True squint is not outgrown. Some babies under 6 months show brief,
inconsistent misalignment that settles; a constant or persisting turn at
any age, or any turn after 6 months, should be examined. Early treatment
protects vision — waiting risks a permanently lazy eye.

## Squint surgery — what to expect

Squint surgery adjusts the tension/position of the eye-moving muscles to
realign the eyes. Points parents ask about: it does NOT involve removing
the eye; it is usually daycare under general anaesthesia in children;
redness lasts a couple of weeks; and glasses/patching often continue after
surgery, because surgery aligns the eyes while glasses/patching handle the
vision part. Adults with long-standing squint can also have alignment
surgery — it restores appearance and confidence and is not merely
"cosmetic"; in some adults it also improves double vision or expands the
visual field.

## Children's eye screening — the schedule

- **At birth / newborn:** red-reflex screen (catches congenital cataract
  and other serious conditions), especially for premature babies —
  premature/low-birth-weight infants need ROP (retinopathy of prematurity)
  screening in the first weeks of life; this is sight-saving and
  time-bound.
- **Around age 3–4:** first full vision check for every child (before
  school), even without complaints.
- **School years:** check every 1–2 years, or immediately with complaints:
  sitting close to the TV/board, squeezing eyes, head tilt, rubbing,
  headaches, falling grades.
- **Any age immediately:** turning eye, white pupil reflection, drooping
  lid covering the pupil, watering with light-sensitivity in an infant
  (possible childhood glaucoma).

## New squint or double vision in adults

A NEW squint or new double vision in an adult is a different problem from
childhood squint — causes include nerve palsies from diabetes or
hypertension, thyroid eye disease, injury, or neurological conditions.
Sudden double vision, especially with headache, droopy lid or unequal
pupils, warrants urgent evaluation the same day.
