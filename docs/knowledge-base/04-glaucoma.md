# Glaucoma (Kala Motia)

## Summary for prompt

Glaucoma is damage to the optic nerve, usually linked to raised eye
pressure. It is called the "silent thief of sight" because the common form
has NO symptoms until much side (peripheral) vision is already lost — and
vision lost to glaucoma can never be brought back. Treatment (drops, laser
or surgery) protects the remaining vision by lowering eye pressure, and is
lifelong. Everyone above 40 — especially with family history of glaucoma,
diabetes, high minus power, or long steroid use — should get eye pressure
and optic nerve checked regularly. A rare acute form causes sudden severe
eye pain, redness, halos and vomiting: that is an emergency — come
immediately. If a patient mentions "kala motia", family history of
glaucoma, or long-term eye-drop use for pressure, encourage regular
follow-up and never stopping drops without the doctor's advice.

## What is glaucoma?

Glaucoma is a group of diseases in which the optic nerve — the cable
carrying vision from the eye to the brain — is slowly damaged, most often
because the fluid pressure inside the eye is higher than that nerve can
tolerate. Nerve fibres are lost from the outside in, so side vision fades
first while central reading vision stays normal until late. Damage is
permanent: treatment can stop or slow further loss but cannot restore what
is gone, which is why early detection matters more in glaucoma than in
almost any other eye disease.

## Types of glaucoma

- **Open-angle glaucoma** — the common, painless, gradual form. No symptoms
  for years; found on screening (pressure, optic nerve exam, visual field
  test, OCT scan).
- **Angle-closure glaucoma** — the drainage angle narrows or shuts. It can
  be chronic, or ACUTE: sudden severe eye pain, headache, redness, blurred
  vision, coloured halos around lights, nausea/vomiting. Acute attack is a
  same-day emergency; a preventive laser (iridotomy) protects narrow-angle
  eyes.
- **Normal-tension glaucoma** — nerve damage despite "normal" pressure;
  managed the same way, by lowering pressure further.
- **Secondary glaucoma** — caused by other conditions: long steroid use
  (drops, skin creams, tablets, inhalers), eye injury, inflammation
  (uveitis), advanced diabetes (neovascular), or a hypermature cataract.
- **Congenital/childhood glaucoma** — a baby with large, hazy, watering
  eyes and light-sensitivity needs urgent paediatric referral.

## Who is at risk and how is it detected?

Higher risk: age above 40, a parent/sibling with glaucoma (family history
raises risk several-fold), diabetes, high myopia (minus power),
hypermetropia (for angle-closure), long-term steroid use in any form, and
past eye injury. Because early glaucoma has no symptoms, detection is by
examination: eye-pressure measurement, optic-nerve examination, gonioscopy
(checking the drainage angle), OCT nerve scans, and visual-field testing.
One normal reading doesn't rule it out; suspects are monitored over time.
A practical rule the bot can share: **after 40, get a full glaucoma
check every 1–2 years — yearly if there's family history or diabetes.**

## Treatment: drops, laser and surgery

Glaucoma treatment lowers eye pressure to a level the nerve can tolerate.

- **Eye drops** — the usual first step; one or more pressure-lowering drops
  used EVERY day, lifelong. Missing drops silently allows damage — the
  disease doesn't remind the patient with symptoms.
- **Laser** — SLT laser can reduce pressure in open-angle glaucoma;
  YAG iridotomy opens a safety channel in narrow angles to prevent acute
  attacks. Both are quick OPD procedures.
- **Surgery (trabeculectomy / drainage implants / MIGS)** — creates a new
  drainage pathway when drops and laser aren't enough. Surgery controls
  pressure; it does not bring back lost vision.

### Q: Can glaucoma be cured? Will my lost vision come back?
A: Glaucoma cannot be cured, and vision already lost cannot be restored —
but with regular treatment and follow-up, most patients keep useful vision
lifelong. That's why the goal is early detection and never stopping
treatment on your own.

### Q: My pressure is normal now — can I stop the drops?
A: No — the pressure is normal BECAUSE of the drops. Stopping them lets the
pressure rise again and damage continue silently. Any change in drops
should only be made by the eye doctor. If drops cause burning, redness or
other trouble, tell the doctor — alternatives exist.

### Q: Is glaucoma related to cataract? Can I have both?
A: They are different diseases that often coexist with age ("safed motia"
= cataract, "kala motia" = glaucoma). Cataract surgery is possible in
glaucoma patients and sometimes even helps pressure; the surgeon plans both
together.

## Living with glaucoma — follow-up rhythm

Typical care: pressure checks every few months, visual fields and OCT about
once or twice a year, and drop refills without gaps. Patients should keep a
list of their drops, use them at fixed times (an alarm helps), press the
inner corner of the eye for a minute after instilling (reduces side
effects), and bring all bottles to appointments. Family members above 40
should also get screened.

## Emergency: acute angle-closure attack

Sudden severe eye pain with redness, misty vision, coloured halos around
lights, headache, nausea or vomiting — especially in the evening or in dim
light — suggests an acute angle-closure attack. This is a same-day
emergency: pressure must be lowered within hours to prevent permanent
damage. The bot should tell such a patient to come to the hospital
immediately (or the nearest emergency eye service if far away).
