# Refractive Errors (Chashma / Glasses Power)

## Summary for prompt

Refractive errors are focusing problems of the eye — myopia (minus power,
distance blur), hypermetropia (plus power), astigmatism (cylinder power,
blur/ghosting at all distances) and presbyopia (age-related near blur after
40). They are corrected with glasses, contact lenses, or surgery (LASIK/
SMILE/ICL for adults with stable power). Children with blurred vision,
squinting, sitting close to the TV or frequent headaches need an eye test —
uncorrected power in childhood can cause permanent lazy eye (amblyopia).
Glasses do not weaken eyes, and using the correct power does not increase
the number. Adults above 40 needing reading glasses is normal (presbyopia).

## What is a refractive error?

A refractive error means the eye cannot focus light exactly onto the
retina, so the image is blurred. It is not a disease — it is a focusing
mismatch between the eye's length and its lens/cornea power. The four
types: **myopia** (nearsightedness — distant objects blur), **hypermetropia**
(farsightedness — near work strains first), **astigmatism** (cylinder power
from an unevenly curved cornea — smearing/ghosting at all distances), and
**presbyopia** (natural age-related loss of near focusing after ~40).
Refractive errors are measured in dioptres ("power" or "number") and
corrected with glasses, contact lenses, or refractive surgery.

## Myopia (minus power / nearsightedness)

In myopia, distant objects look blurred while near objects stay clear. It
usually starts in school years and can increase until the early 20s.
Signs in children: sitting close to the TV/board, squeezing eyes to see
far, copying mistakes from the blackboard, headaches. Correction is with
minus-power glasses or contact lenses; adults with stable power can
consider LASIK/SMILE/ICL. **High myopia** (roughly −6 D and above) needs
periodic retina check-ups — such eyes have a higher lifetime risk of
retinal thinning, holes and detachment, independent of any surgery.
Outdoor time in daylight is associated with slower myopia progression in
children; specialised options for slowing progression (special lenses or
drops) exist and should be discussed with the doctor.

## Hypermetropia (plus power / farsightedness)

In hypermetropia the eye is optically "short", so focusing needs extra
effort — near work blurs or strains first, and in higher powers distance
also blurs. Children can often compensate by focusing harder, which may
cause headaches, eye strain, or an inward-turning squint; significant plus
power in a child must be corrected to prevent lazy eye. Correction is with
plus-power glasses or contact lenses; selected adults may be candidates for
refractive surgery.

## Astigmatism (cylinder power)

Astigmatism means the cornea (or lens) is curved unevenly — shaped more
like a spoon than a ball — so light forms a smeared image. Symptoms:
blur or double/ghost images at all distances, eye strain, headaches, tilting
the head to see better. Small amounts are extremely common and normal.
Correction is with cylindrical glasses, toric contact lenses, or surgery;
during cataract surgery it can be corrected with a toric IOL. A rapidly
increasing cylinder in a young person needs a keratoconus check (corneal
scan) — see the cornea document.

## Presbyopia (age-related reading problem)

From around age 40, the eye's natural lens stiffens and near focusing
weakens — small print gets pushed farther away, and reading in dim light
becomes hard. This is presbyopia; it happens to everyone, including people
who never wore glasses, and it progresses till about 60. Correction:
reading glasses, bifocals or progressive lenses, multifocal contact lenses,
or surgical options (discussed case-by-case). Presbyopia is not a disease
and does not damage the eye.

### Q: Do glasses weaken the eyes or increase the number?
A: No. Glasses simply focus light correctly; they neither weaken the eye
nor increase the power. The number changes because the eye itself grows or
ages — with or without glasses. NOT wearing the correct glasses harms
children specifically, because a blurred image can cause permanent lazy eye
(amblyopia) in the developing brain.

### Q: Can eye exercises, diets or drops remove glasses?
A: No exercise, food or drop has been scientifically proven to remove a
genuine glasses power. Exercises may help certain muscle-coordination
problems (convergence issues), but they do not change myopia,
hypermetropia or astigmatism. Be cautious of such claims; surgical options
(LASIK/SMILE/ICL) are the proven route for adults who want freedom from
glasses.

### Q: How often should power be checked?
A: Children: every 6–12 months (power changes as they grow). Adults with
stable vision: every 1–2 years, or sooner if vision changes. Diabetics and
those above 40 should use the visit to also screen eye pressure and retina.

## Contact lenses — safe use

Contact lenses are a safe alternative to glasses when used hygienically:
wash and dry hands before handling; never sleep, shower or swim in lenses
(unless specifically designed and advised); never use tap water on lenses;
replace lenses and cases on schedule; and stop wearing them immediately if
the eye becomes red, painful or light-sensitive — continuing can turn a
minor irritation into a serious corneal infection. Anyone with a red,
painful eye while using contact lenses should be seen urgently.

## Children and school screening

Any child who fails a school vision test, squints/tilts the head, sits too
close to screens, rubs eyes often, or complains of headaches should have a
complete eye exam with cycloplegic refraction (drops that relax focusing —
they blur vision for a few hours, which is normal). Correcting power early
protects the child from amblyopia (lazy eye), which becomes hard to treat
after ages 8–10. Spectacles prescribed to a child should be worn full-time
as advised.
